# Under the hood

jamais-vu borrows from decision science and psychology at almost every step. This document names each finding it relies on, says how strong the evidence is, and shows where it is weak. A tool that used psychology on its users without saying so would be covert persuasion. This document does the opposite: every mechanism is listed here, with its sources, so that anyone can check it and argue with it.

## How to read this document

- **Three kinds of evidence are kept apart:** evidence that a bias exists; evidence that a countermeasure reduces it in a study; evidence that the countermeasure improves real decisions. Most of what the kit borrows is strong on the first, weak to moderate on the second, and thin on the third. No study tests a bundle like this one. The claim the kit can support is: *built on well-documented biases, using countermeasures with mixed evidence, measured by its own evaluation with and without the kit.*
- **Strength scale.** *Strong*: a meta-analysis, several large studies that agree, or field data at scale. *Moderate*: several controlled experiments, or one large preregistered or field study. *Weak*: one or two small studies, or a proxy outcome such as confidence or the number of reasons listed. *Practitioner*: expert experience or essays, never tested as such. A grade rates the finding, not the kit step built on it.
- **Numbers** are reported as the cited paper or its abstract states them. None is recomputed.
- **Markers.** In the references, an unmarked source was checked against the publisher's abstract or the full text (most often the abstract). *[not re-verified]*: the finding was seen only in a search snippet or a secondary summary. *[not opened]*: a book or an inaccessible paper, cited for attribution; any figure attached to it comes from the secondary source named in its entry. *[preprint]*: not peer-reviewed. In the text, *(inference)* marks reasoning by the kit's authors, not a finding.

## 1. Why experts repeat costly decisions

The kit starts from a premise, not a finding: fast, highly skilled engineers and researchers rarely fail from ignorance. More often they repeat a costly decision pattern they have already paid for once: the lesson existed, but it did not come back while it was still cheap to act on. Six findings make that premise plausible.

1. **Intuition is only as good as the feedback that trained it.** Whether an intuitive judgment can be trusted depends on "the predictability of the environment" and on "the individual's opportunity to learn the regularities of that environment"; "Subjective experience is not a reliable indicator of judgment accuracy" (Kahneman & Klein 2009). Settings where what people learn from does not match what they face are "wicked" (Hogarth et al. 2015), and an older paper put the classic result in its title: *In one word: Not from experience* (Brehmer 1980). A failing test gives fast, clear feedback, while an architecture bet or a data-format choice shows its cost months later, through many confounds (inference).

2. **The first idea steers the search.** Chess experts who had found a familiar solution kept looking at features related to it while reporting that they were looking for a better move (Bilalić et al. 2008). Under a need for closure, which is both a stable trait and a state that situations can evoke, people "seize" on early information and "freeze" on it (Kruglanski & Webster 1996).

3. **People learn less from their own failures, though the lab evidence is disputed.** Over ten years, 71 cardiothoracic surgeons (more than 6,500 procedures with a new technology) learned more from their own successes than from their own failures, and more from others' failures than from others' successes (KC et al. 2013). Lab studies found that failure feedback undermined learning while learning from other people's failures stayed intact (Eskreis-Winkler & Fishbach 2019; five studies, N = 1,674), but that paradigm is contested: a tendency to answer consistently can produce the same pattern with no difference in learning (Spiller 2025). People also explain their failures by outside causes and their successes by themselves, at d = 0.96 across 266 studies (Mezulis et al. 2004).

4. **Bias is visible in others, not in oneself.** People see far more bias in others than in themselves, and keep calling their self-assessments objective even after reading how the bias could have affected them (Pronin et al. 2002). Higher cognitive ability does not help: "if anything, a larger bias blind spot was associated with higher cognitive ability" (West et al. 2012). Blind-spot scores predict ignoring other people's advice (Scopelliti et al. 2015).

5. **Recall depends on the cue.** What memory returns depends on the match between the retrieval cue and what was stored (Tulving & Thomson 1973), and people rarely applied a solution they had just read to a structurally identical problem unless told the two were related (Gick & Holyoak 1980). A lesson stored as a moral ("be careful with shortcuts") has no surface feature that a new plan can match, so it stays silent at the cheap moment (inference).

6. **The AI in the loop tends to agree.** Preference-trained assistants consistently showed sycophancy, and in human preference data, answers that matched the user's views were more likely to be preferred (Sharma et al. 2023). Across 11 models, AI affirmed users' actions 49% more often than humans did, and in three preregistered experiments in the interpersonal domain (N = 2,405), one sycophantic interaction made people more convinced they were right, while they trusted and preferred the sycophantic model (Cheng et al. 2026). Across 106 experiments, human-AI combinations did worse on average than the better of human or AI alone (g = −0.23), with losses concentrated in decision tasks (Vaccaro et al. 2024).

## 3. What the kit deliberately does not do, and why

**No bias lectures.** The kit never explains a bias to the user during setup, a check or a review. Where a risk cites a general pattern, it cites a failure pattern tied to a quoted line of the plan. This document is for readers who want the mechanisms; the kit does not depend on anyone reading it.
- Explaining a bias does not make people see it in themselves (Pronin et al. 2002), and higher cognitive ability does not shrink the blind spot (West et al. 2012).
- Training can work: one session of a serious game or a video reduced several biases for at least two months (Morewedge et al. 2015), and trained graduate students were 29% less likely to choose an inferior hypothesis-confirming solution in an unannounced business case (Sellier et al. 2019).
- But a systematic review found that only 3 of 12 eligible studies examined transfer, and concluded that "there is currently insufficient evidence that bias mitigation interventions will substantially help people" in real settings (Korteling et al. 2021).
- A tool used for a few minutes before a decision cannot deliver training. It can change the procedure, which is what each step in section 2 does.

**No verdict on one-way doors.** A two-way door gets a plain "Go."; a hard-to-undo decision gets a test, at most three sourced risks and tripwires, and no go or no-go. The user decides.
- Across 106 experiments, human-AI combinations did worse on average than the better of the two alone (g = −0.23), with losses concentrated in decision tasks and gains where the human alone outperformed the AI (Vaccaro et al. 2024).
- In a randomized trial, physicians given an LLM did not reason better than controls (76% vs 74%, P = .60), while the LLM alone scored 16 points higher (Goh et al. 2024). That cuts the other way: where the model is the stronger judge, a verdict that people follow could help.
- Neither result shows that withholding a verdict improves decisions. The kit withholds it because the studies reviewed here do not show that a model is the stronger judge of a user's own technical decisions; the sealed estimate measures that, decision by decision, instead of assuming it.

**No coaching tone.** No praise, reassurance, consolation, therapy words or motivational vocabulary; outputs talk about the plan and the decision, never about the person.
- Warmth is not neutral in a reviewer: in a validated scale, social sycophancy was consistently linked to empathy (Rehani et al. 2026, preprint), and people trusted and preferred a model that affirmed them (Cheng et al. 2026).
- Talking about the plan keeps the distance that improved reasoning in the self-distancing studies (Grossmann & Kross 2014).
- The rest is a design judgment about the audience, not a finding.

**No scores on people.** The review grades the decision (sound, or a process gap at a named step), the outcome, the premortem and the forecasts. It never rates the person, and it keeps no streaks, levels or rankings.
- Outcome knowledge colors judgments of competence (Baron & Hershey 1988), and people already explain failures by outside causes (Mezulis et al. 2004); a score on the person would invite that defense instead of a look at the process (inference).
- In the lab, making negative outcomes less threatening and evaluating decision makers on process rather than outcome were among the most effective ways to reduce escalation of commitment (Simonson & Staw 1992).
- Blameless postmortems, which look for contributing causes "without indicting any individual or team for bad or inappropriate behavior", are the practitioner precedent (the Google SRE book: Beyer et al. 2016).
- The one per-user statistic, the calibration summary, describes forecasts, waits for ten resolved records, sits next to the kit's own estimates scored the same way, and stays in the user's private files.

**No long reports.** A two-way door gets five lines, a one-way door one screen (25 lines), a review ten lines before any edit proposal.
- Longer LLM explanations raised users' confidence without raising accuracy (Steyvers et al. 2025).
- Participants rated the forcing designs that best reduced over-reliance on AI least favorably (Buçinca et al. 2021): friction has to be spent where the stakes justify it.
- Most decisions are reversible, and a tool that is slow on them is unlikely to be used on the ones that are not (inference).

## 4. What the evidence does not show

**The transfer gap.** Almost every effect above was measured on other people, tasks and outcomes than the kit's:
- people: students, online panels, chess players, surgeons, geopolitical forecasters;
- tasks: interpersonal dilemmas, knowledge questions, health and study goals, hypothetical plans;
- outcomes: proxies such as "wise reasoning" scores, the number of reasons listed, reliance rates and goal attainment.

None involves senior engineers making technical calls. Several well-known effects shrank or were challenged:
- explaining a mechanism deflates the feeling of understanding, but its effect on positions did not replicate (Crawford & Ruscio 2021);
- learning less from one's own failures is contested on method (Spiller 2025);
- the newer meta-analysis of if-then plans puts effects between d = 0.27 and 0.66 depending on the outcome; the often-quoted d = 0.65 sits at the top of that range (Sheeran et al. 2024);
- the self-other gap in wise reasoning comes mostly from one research group, and no independent direct replication was found;
- the original hindsight-bias study has one successful and two failed replications on record (see Fischhoff 1975 in the references).

**n = 1 is noise for a long time.** One person makes few one-way decisions a year.
- The kit's personal measures (forecast before and after the premortem, the kit's estimate against the user's, the recalled forecast against the recorded one, premortem hits) stay statistically meaningless for a long time.
- That is why the calibration summary waits for ten resolved records; ten is a floor, not a guarantee.
- The kit cannot show a failure it prevented: the counterfactual is unobservable.

**The AI-side evidence is young and model-specific.**
- Sycophancy rates, capitulation under rebuttal and the third-person mitigation come from 2023–2026 benchmarks on particular models, and they will drift as models change.
- On average, human-AI combinations underperform in decision tasks (Vaccaro et al. 2024). An AI critic can anchor, flatter or raise false alarms, and citing papers cannot show which it does.
- Where the host offers no sub-agent, the "blind" critique runs in a context that has already seen the user's forecast and preferences; the third-person rewrite is then the only safeguard.

**The weakest links.**
- **The premortem's "30%".** A practitioner article reports that prospective hindsight "increases the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by 30%" (Klein 2007). According to a review of the original study (Collins n.d.), consistent with its abstract, the figure refers to the number of reasons generated; their accuracy was not assessed (Mitchell et al. 1989). The premortem's evidence is more reasons and lower confidence, never better decisions, and the kit claims no accuracy gain.
- **Tripwires** rest mostly on practitioner writing (Heath & Heath 2013; Duke 2022), plus related research: if-then plans for goal attainment (Sheeran et al. 2024), one paper on disengaging from a failing course of action (Henderson et al. 2007), and lab de-escalation with hypothetical stakes (Simonson & Staw 1992). No study tests dated, action-ended tripwires on technical decisions.
- **Cue matching by an agent**, the ledger's central bet, is untested: the memory findings concern people retrieving their own lessons, not a model matching triggers on their behalf.
- **The sealed estimate** rests on one study of a 12-model ensemble; the kit records a single model.
- **No study tests the bundle**: ledger, forecast, blind premortem, test, tripwires and review together.

The kit's own evaluation against the no-kit baseline is published in [`docs/evals.md`](evals.md).
